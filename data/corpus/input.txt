The old lighthouse stood at the edge of the cliff, its lamp long dark, its keeper long gone.
Every evening the fishermen would look up at the empty tower and remember the light that used to guide them home.

Marta had grown up in the village below. As a child she would climb the winding stairs
with her grandfather, who tended the lamp before the automated beacons took his job away.
He used to say that a light only matters if someone is watching for it, and that the sea
does not forgive a sailor who trusts to luck instead of care.

One winter the storms came harder than anyone remembered. The new beacon on the far point
failed in the wind, and three boats were caught out past the reef with no way to find the
harbor mouth. Marta climbed the lighthouse stairs in the dark, found the old lamp still oiled
and ready, and lit it herself for the first time in a decade.

The boats turned toward the light. One by one they came in through the gap in the reef,
guided by nothing more than an old flame in an old glass and a woman who remembered how
her grandfather had held the match.

In the morning the village gathered at the base of the tower. No one asked her to keep the
light burning, but she did anyway, every night, whether the automated beacon was working or not.
She said the sea had taught her grandfather one true thing: it is better to have two lights
than to trust that one will never fail.

Years later, when Marta herself grew old, a young boy from the village began climbing the
stairs with her in the evenings, watching how she filled the lamp, how she trimmed the wick,
how she waited for dusk before she lit it. He did not know yet that he was learning the same
lesson she had learned as a child: that some jobs are not about being needed every day,
they are about being ready for the one night when you are needed most.

The lighthouse still stands at the edge of the cliff. Its lamp still burns most evenings,
tended now by the boy who is no longer a boy. The automated beacon on the far point has
been replaced twice since the storm. The old lamp has not been replaced at all.

"Why do you still light it," the boy asked one evening, "when the automated beacon works
fine most nights?" Marta smiled: some questions, she said, don't have a one-sentence answer.
There are 12 boats out past the reef tonight, and every single one of them matters!

It was a hard-won lesson; not every night needs a hero, but every night needs a light.
Well, she added, isn't that always the way? You keep the lamp burning for the one night
in a hundred when it's the only thing standing between a boat and the rocks.
